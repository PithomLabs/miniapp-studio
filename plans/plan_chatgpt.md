# Miniapp Studio — Consolidated Product, Architecture, Connector, and Hacksgiving Plan

**Version:** 1.0  
**Date:** 24 November 2026  
**Status:** Strategic consolidation and hackathon scope proposal; not yet an implementation specification  
**Product:** Miniapp Studio  
**Foundation:** `github.com/PithomLabs/miniapp` (pre-existing Miniapp framework; pin the exact revision used by the submission)

---

## 1. Executive decision

Build **Miniapp Studio**, an AI-native application factory for business users. A user describes a business need; Studio turns it into a versioned, declarative Miniapp definition composed from approved data, UI, workflow, connector, and policy primitives. Studio validates the definition, presents the required capabilities and permissions, previews the generated application, and runs it through a constrained generic runtime.

The product is **not** twelve separately hand-built business agents. The earlier twelve concepts become reusable primitives, gallery templates, and proof cases for one application-generation platform.

The existing Miniapp Go framework is the execution foundation. The visible Hacksgiving product is the Studio UI, the generation/validation loop, the connector directory and setup flow, and one working generated application running with a real Sprites connector from a Fly.io Sprite.

### Product promise

> **Describe the business application you need. Miniapp Studio generates it from reusable primitives, connects it to the systems you authorize, and lets you inspect what it will do before it runs.**

### Strategic differentiator — roadmap, not a Hacksgiving claim

Miniapp's long-term differentiation is **durable business-workflow execution**: generated applications should eventually support long-running processes that survive service and worker restarts, wait for people or events, retry safely, resume from recorded workflow state, reconcile uncertain external effects, and preserve an auditable history.

This is a product vision and roadmap direction. It is **not part of the Hacksgiving demo unless each specific behavior is implemented and verified**. The demo must not imply that a filesystem checkpoint is a durable business-workflow checkpoint or that restoring a Sprite undoes external API effects.

## 2. Product thesis and target user

### 2.1 What Miniapp Studio is

Miniapp Studio is a generic, user-facing application builder. It gives non-programmers a controlled set of primitives and a natural-language interface to create small business applications without writing Go or designing every application from scratch.

Users should be able to generate applications such as:

- Customer and sales workspaces.
- Invoice review and payment follow-up tools.
- Employee task and onboarding applications.
- Inventory and order tracking views.
- Project and issue triage workspaces.
- Operational dashboards and reporting tools.

These are examples and templates, not separate codebases. They should be generated by the same Studio, renderer, connector layer, and execution runtime.

### 2.2 Initial target market

Start with **business owners, operators, and small teams** that use several SaaS services but do not have the budget or engineering staff to commission a custom internal application for every workflow.

A later distribution channel can embed or white-label Studio inside other SaaS products, allowing those vendors' customers to generate applications over the vendor's approved APIs. Do not make white-label embedding or a multi-tenant SaaS marketplace a Hacksgiving requirement.

### 2.3 Product boundaries

| Miniapp Studio owns | External systems own |
|---|---|
| Application definitions and versions | Their underlying business records |
| Generic UI and app composition | Their native user experience |
| Capability and operation catalog | Provider APIs and provider-specific limits |
| Application-level authorization and policy | Provider authentication and account grants |
| Workflow definitions and execution coordination | External side effects and their actual outcomes |
| Validation, preview, run records, and audit | Provider data and service availability |

Miniapp should remain provider-agnostic at the business-logic layer. A business operation such as `customer.find` or `invoice.create_draft` should not be hardcoded to one CRM or accounting product. Provider adapters map that logical operation to the available connector capabilities.

## 3. Consolidated design principles

1. **Generate applications, not one-off agents.** Every gallery example must be assembled from the same platform primitives.
2. **Generate specifications, not arbitrary programs.** The model emits a constrained declarative application definition. A trusted, fixed interpreter renders and runs it. Do not execute unreviewed generated Go, JavaScript, shell, or arbitrary code in the trusted runtime.
3. **One generic runner, many application definitions.** Runtime-generated applications are data interpreted by a generic runner; they are not separately compiled Miniapp binaries.
4. **Validate deterministically.** Schema checks, capability checks, permission checks, static safety rules, and fixture-based dry runs must be performed by code, not accepted merely because an LLM says the definition is valid.
5. **Discover capabilities dynamically.** The Studio must discover the connectors and operations available to the current organization and Sprite, and must not pretend a provider is connected just because it appears in the public catalog.
6. **Keep credentials out of generated specifications and Sprite filesystems.** Use the Sprites API gateway for connector calls. Do not put raw provider tokens in prompts, app specs, logs, generated files, or agent environment variables.
7. **Least privilege by default.** Generated apps declare exactly which connectors and operations they need. Access is denied until explicitly granted. Apply organization, connector, Sprite, path, and operation boundaries wherever the APIs support them.
8. **Separate preview from execution.** Preview and dry-run must not silently perform external writes.
9. **Human confirmation is a platform policy, not a harness feature.** For the demo, use a simple explicit confirmation before any consequential write that is included. Longer-lived approval inboxes and out-of-band approval workflows belong to the roadmap.
10. **State claims at the level of evidence.** Do not promise general undo, exactly-once external effects, durable waits, or automatic recovery unless the pinned version and the exact path have passing tests.

### 4.2 Hacksgiving placement decision

For the Hacksgiving prototype, use **one authorized personal Sprite** for the builder agent and the generated app runner/preview, unless an early integration spike proves that a different split is necessary. This avoids the extra work of provisioning one Sprite for every generated demo app.

The builder agent must run on a Sprite and make at least one real call through a Sprites connector. The Studio should visibly demonstrate the connection and the result.

Do not put a broad Fly organization token or raw provider credentials into the builder agent. The trusted Studio control plane should own privileged Sprite lifecycle and connector-management operations. The Sprite-hosted agent should receive only the capabilities required for its task.

### 4.3 Longer-term placement policy

Do not hardcode “one Sprite per app” as a universal rule. Placement should follow trust, isolation, lifecycle, and cost requirements:

- A personal builder agent may have a persistent personal/workspace Sprite.
- A bounded untrusted build or preview task may use an isolated or disposable Sprite.
- Trusted production Miniapp execution may remain on Fly Machines.
- Durable business workflows may orchestrate bounded Sprite tasks without confusing Sprite filesystem state with workflow state.

The placement policy is a later platform capability; the Hacksgiving demo only needs one real Sprite path.

## 5. The declarative Miniapp specification

### 5.1 Core rule

The LLM generates a versioned application definition, not a deployable program. The definition references only known components, logical data sources, registered operations, allowed workflow steps, and declared policies.

A generated app is therefore:

- Readable and inspectable by a non-programmer.
- Schema-validatable before execution.
- Diffable and versionable.
- Bound to a known set of connector operations and policies.
- Executable by one fixed interpreter.

A run should pin the exact immutable spec version or content hash it started with. Editing an application creates a new version; it must not silently mutate the definition of a running instance.

### 5.2 Suggested top-level sections

| Section | Contains |
|---|---|
| `app` | Name, description, version, owner/workspace, lifecycle status |
| `data` | Logical entities, fields, relationships, query definitions, source bindings |
| `ui` | Pages, navigation, components, component properties, data bindings, actions |
| `workflows` | Trigger declarations and typed, bounded steps |
| `capabilities` | Required logical operations and provider/connector bindings |
| `policies` | Roles, permitted actions, approval requirement, rate/action limits |
| `fixtures` | Representative test data and expected validation results |
| `presentation` | Theme, labels, layout, branding tokens |

### 5.3 Example — illustrative only

```yaml
app:
  id: invoice-review
  name: Invoice Review
  version: 1

data:
  sources:
    - id: invoices
      capability: documents.invoice.read
    - id: purchase-orders
      capability: purchasing.order.read

ui:
  pages:
    - id: overview
      components:
        - type: metric-card
          title: Invoices awaiting review
          binding: invoices.pending_count
        - type: table
          binding: invoices.pending
          on_select: invoice-detail
    - id: invoice-detail
      components:
        - type: record-detail
          binding: invoices.selected
        - type: comparison-panel
          left: invoices.selected
          right: purchase-orders.match
        - type: action-button
          action: prepare-review

workflows:
  - id: prepare-review
    trigger: user_action
    steps:
      - type: capability.read
        operation: purchasing.order.match
      - type: transform
        operation: compare_invoice_and_order
      - type: present_result

policies:
  external_writes: disabled
  unregistered_operations: deny
```

This is a design sketch, not a frozen schema or a claim that these operations already exist.

### 5.4 Minimal workflow language

Keep the interpreter deliberately small. The eventual vocabulary can include:

- Triggers: manual, schedule, webhook, provider event.
- Steps: connector call, model call, transform, condition/branch, bounded loop, notify, approve, wait, finish.
- References: typed outputs from prior steps, with schema validation.
- Controls: maximum iterations, timeouts, allowed operations, failure behavior.

For Hacksgiving, support only the subset that can be implemented and tested end to end: a manual trigger, connector read, bounded transform/model step if available, and UI result. A basic confirm-before-write interaction can be added only if the write path is genuinely wired. Durable waits, scheduled execution, restart recovery, retries across long waits, sagas, and resumable approvals are roadmap work.

- Detail view and detail card.
- Form and input controls.
- Chart and summary panel.
- Status, empty, loading, and error states.
- Action button and contextual menu.
- Run status and progress view.
- Permissions/connection summary.
- Basic confirmation panel for consequential demo actions.

### 6.3 Workflow primitives

- Manual trigger.
- Connector operation.
- Model/AI operation (through a permitted model connector).
- Pure transform.
- Condition/branch.
- Bounded loop.
- Notify.
- Finish/result.

**Roadmap additions:** scheduled and event triggers, durable wait, human approval that parks and resumes, retry/backoff, cancellation, compensation, entity serialization, deadlines, and long-running workflow history.

### 6.4 Connector and capability primitives

- Discover provider catalog.
- Discover organization-configured connections.
- Select a connection for an application.
- Discover/document allowed operations.
- Create/test a Custom API connector.
- Bind a logical business capability to one provider operation.
- Declare the exact request paths and effect type used by an app.

A connector is not automatically a complete business capability. For a custom API, Miniapp must separately describe the allowed HTTP operations, request and response schemas, business meaning, and read/write effect classification. Do not expose an unrestricted arbitrary-URL tool to generated apps.

### 6.5 Governance primitives

- Required connector and operation list.
- Role/action mapping.
- Least-privilege connection policy.
- Read-only versus write classification.
- User confirmation for demo writes.
- Action and run limits.
- Stop/disable control where implemented.
- Validation report and run evidence.

**Roadmap additions:** durable approval inbox, out-of-band approval identity, immutable effect ledger, UNKNOWN-outcome quarantine, reconcile-by-read, compensation/saga, and organization-wide policy management.

## 7. Fly Sprites connector architecture

### 7.1 Three distinct connector views in Studio

The Studio must never use one ambiguous “connected” status. It must distinguish:

1. **Public Fly ecosystem catalog:** providers and connector types Fly publicly lists as available in the Sprites ecosystem.
2. **Organization connections:** connections actually configured for the authenticated Fly organization.
3. **Sprite/app authorization:** which configured connection and request paths are permitted for the specific Sprite and generated application.

The Fly API documentation describes `GET /v1/oauth/connections` as listing connectors configured for the authenticated organization, with an optional provider filter. It is not a public, global catalog of every provider Fly supports. Therefore Studio should maintain a versioned metadata catalog based on Fly's public ecosystem directory and reconcile it with live organization connections.

### 7.2 Connector directory UI

For every public catalog entry, display:

- Provider and connector name.
- Category (Studio-defined taxonomy; not a claim about Fly's internal categories).
- Authentication type shown by Fly (OAuth, API key/BYOK, token, service account, etc.).
- Short supported-purpose description from the ecosystem listing.
- Maintainer marker where Fly labels the connector as Community.
- Public documentation link.
- Organization status: not configured, configured, or status unavailable.
- Sprite authorization status: denied, allowed, or unknown.
- App dependency status: required by this app or not used.

Recommended status vocabulary:

- `Listed by Fly` — present in the public ecosystem catalog.
- `Not configured` — absent from the organization's live connections.
- `Configured for organization` — returned by the organization connections API.
- `Allowed for this Sprite` — current connector access policy permits this Sprite.
- `Test passed` — a supported connection test has succeeded.
- `Needs attention` — test failed, policy missing, or required permission absent.
- `Community-maintained` — explicitly marked Community in Fly's ecosystem listing.

Do not display “available now” or “connected” merely because a provider appears in the public directory. Fly notes that provider availability can vary by organization during rollout; the organization dashboard/live API is authoritative for actual enablement.

### 7.4 Custom API connectors are a first-class requirement

Miniapp Studio must support a user adding a token-authenticated HTTP API that is not in Fly's built-in provider list. The Studio must not be limited to a fixed set of prebuilt integrations.

The current Fly Custom API connector flow documents:

- `POST /v1/oauth/connections/custom_api` to create a custom API connector.
- Required connector identity (`name`), base API URL, and access token.
- Configurable authentication placement: `header`, `url_path`, `query_param`, or `custom_header`.
- Optional custom header name, authorization prefix, query parameter name, URL path template, description, and refresh token.
- A stored test URL; creation itself does not run the test.
- A separate custom API test endpoint.
- Credentials stored encrypted and not returned by the API.
- Empty or missing initial access policy denies Sprite use until access is granted.
- Creating another custom API connector with the same name updates that connector in place; the Studio should make this behavior obvious and prevent accidental replacement.

The API operations require the appropriate Fly organization authorization (the create/test documentation specifies full access). These are privileged control-plane operations: perform them through the trusted Studio backend after an authorized user action. Do not hand full-access Fly tokens to the Sprite-hosted generator.

The custom connector request path must be tested before the connection is treated as ready. The documentation distinguishes connector creation from connection testing; Studio should represent those as separate states.

A Custom API connector only establishes a gateway-managed connection to a token-authenticated HTTP API. It does not automatically create safe business operations. Miniapp must register a constrained operation schema for each operation the generated app may call.

### 7.5 Credential and network boundaries

- Collect a provider secret only in a secure Studio/backend setup flow.
- Never include the raw credential in the generated Miniapp specification.
- Never render or return secret values after creation.
- Never pass a broad Fly organization token to the generated agent.
- Use per-Sprite connector access policies; default to deny.
- Where supported, restrict connector access by Sprite name/labels and permitted request paths.
- Register only approved operations in Miniapp's capability registry.
- Keep custom API upstreams constrained to the documented gateway behavior; do not add a generic unrestricted proxy or arbitrary URL fetch step.
- Redact credentials, authorization headers, and token-bearing URLs from logs and run timelines.

## 11. Durable workflow — core product differentiator, post-hackathon roadmap

Durable workflow is the strategic reason Miniapp can become more than a no-code UI builder. It should be developed deliberately after the Hacksgiving prototype, with explicit correctness gates.

### 11.1 Intended user-facing capabilities

Generated Miniapps should eventually support:

- Manual, scheduled, webhook, and provider-event triggers.
- Long-running waits and event signals.
- Human approvals that survive browser, agent, and worker restarts.
- Retry and backoff with explicit idempotency identities.
- Cancellation and deadline handling.
- Step-by-step execution history and evidence.
- Reconciliation of uncertain external API outcomes.
- Compensation for effects that can be reversed by a separate operation.
- Version-safe workflow continuation after app upgrades.
- Entity-level serialization where concurrent operations must not conflict.

### 11.2 Correct execution semantics

Do not claim exactly-once external effects. The honest target is:

- At-least-once execution of workflow steps where appropriate.
- Stable idempotency identities for business effects.
- No duplicate **confirmed** business effects when the provider and adapter support the required reconciliation.
- No silently lost operations.
- No blind retry after an unknown provider outcome.
- UNKNOWN outcomes are quarantined and reconciled by reading the provider or routed to a human.

### 11.3 Separate three kinds of state

1. **Application definition state:** immutable versioned Miniapp specification.
2. **Business workflow state:** durable steps, timers, signals, approvals, and effect records (the Miniapp durable runtime roadmap).
3. **Sprite filesystem state:** files and environment captured by Fly Sprite checkpoints.

A Sprite checkpoint restores filesystem state. It does not rewind a DBOS workflow, erase an external Slack message, reverse a payment, or undo a third-party API write. Any future “Rewind” UX must combine a precisely defined restore with effect reconciliation/compensation and clearly label irreversible actions.

### 11.4 Roadmap sequence

**Phase A — Hacksgiving foundation**

Studio UI, declarative app definition, schema validator, generic renderer/runner, connector catalog and Custom API path, one personal Sprite, one real connector-backed application.

**Phase B — Productize the builder**

Persistent app registry/versioning, reusable app templates, richer data and UI primitives, capability metadata, connector operation schemas, app-specific connector bindings, user roles, publish/clone/edit lifecycle, and better diagnostics.

**Phase C — Durable workflow runtime**

Integrate Miniapp's durable execution substrate with generated app plans. Add durable waits/signals, approvals, retries, cancellation, idempotency, effect ledger, UNKNOWN quarantine, reconciliation, compensation, and crash/restart conformance tests. Keep execution class and compute placement as separate dimensions.

**Phase D — Ecosystem and distribution**

More provider adapters, connector capability packs, import from OpenAPI, white-label embedding, teams/tenant administration, template sharing, version promotion, and controlled app distribution.

## 12. Submission honesty and source-of-work disclosure

The Hacksgiving brief says the project must be built during Hacksgiving week. Because the Miniapp framework predates the event, the submission should clearly distinguish:

## 13. Technical integration guidance

### 13.1 Go and Sprite management

Use the official `github.com/superfly/sprites-go` SDK behind a Miniapp-owned interface for Sprite operations such as command execution and the management surfaces it supports. Pin the SDK revision. Its current public README specifies Go 1.26.0 or newer; verify compatibility before updating the Miniapp Go toolchain.

### 13.2 Connector management

Implement a typed Go REST client for the Sprites Connectors API where the SDK does not expose the needed endpoint. Keep the REST client behind a narrow interface and test it with fakes and contract tests.

Initial API surface to verify against the pinned Fly API documentation:

- List organization-configured connectors: `GET /v1/oauth/connections`.
- Filter by provider where useful.
- Create Custom API connector: `POST /v1/oauth/connections/custom_api`.
- Test a Custom API connector using the documented test endpoint.
- Read/update connector details and access policies only where the required authorization and endpoint contracts are verified.

The connector catalog itself should be a separate versioned metadata file or registry. Do not populate the public catalog solely from `GET /v1/oauth/connections`, because that endpoint returns only connections configured for the authenticated organization.

### 13.3 Capability metadata for Custom API

For every custom endpoint operation, store a Miniapp-owned operation descriptor containing:

- Stable logical capability ID.
- Connection ID reference (never a secret).
- HTTP method and allowed relative path.
- Request schema and response schema.
- Read/write/effect classification.
- Required approval or role.
- Rate/action limits.
- Timeout and maximum response size.
- Idempotency/reconciliation notes (for roadmap writes).

The runtime must reject paths, methods, or operations not declared by the app's validated specification and authorized capability set.

## answer

Yes. I think demonstrating DBOS durable workflows could be your strongest technical differentiator—but I would make it the second act of the demonstration, not the opening pitch.

There is an important distinction:
- Miniapp Studio wins attention by generating an application from a business description.
- Miniapp wins technical credibility by showing that the generated application can execute consequential business processes reliably.
- DBOS makes the latter particularly interesting because it gives you a story beyond ordinary AI application builders.
My recommendation is to demonstrate durable execution as the technology that makes generated applications trustworthy, not as a DBOS product demonstration.
1. Why DBOS could give you an advantage
Most application generators can demonstrate something visually impressive: describe an application, generate a dashboard, populate a table and show a workflow.

But that does not establish that the generated application can reliably execute a business process.

Consider this example:

A user creates an invoice-management application and defines this workflow:
1. Detect an incoming invoice.
2. Extract its details.
3. Match it against a purchase order.
4. Request manager approval.
5. Send a payment instruction after approval.
6. Record the result.
Now imagine the Sprite or worker crashes while the process is waiting for approval.

A conventional demonstration might restart the process and begin again. A durable workflow should recover its execution state and continue from the appropriate point, subject to the correctness of its recorded external effects.

That is a much more interesting capability to demonstrate.

You are not just generating applications. You are generating applications capable of running long-lived business processes.
2. Why I would not make DBOS the headline
There are three reasons.

A. It is not what the hackathon is asking you to demonstrate.

Fly's actual requirements are that you build a personal agent that:
- Runs on a Sprite.
- Uses at least one Sprites connector.
- Is built during Hacksgiving week.
The published brief does not require a durable workflow engine or a particular orchestration technology. 

Fly

Your strongest differentiator for this particular competition is the combination of:
1. An agent that generates applications.
2. Generic, reusable application primitives.
3. Live integration with the Sprites connector ecosystem.
4. Deterministic validation before execution.
5. A useful, polished Studio experience.
A DBOS demonstration could consume time that would otherwise improve those visible product capabilities.

B. DBOS is infrastructure, not the user-facing magic.

A business owner does not necessarily care whether an application uses DBOS, River, or another execution engine.

What they care about is:

"I created an application in five minutes, and it can reliably handle my business processes without me having to manage every step."

DBOS helps you deliver that promise eventually. It is not itself the promise.

C. A failed durability demonstration could hurt more than help.

DBOS has real technical subtleties.

Its Go documentation explicitly states that workflow steps execute at least once. A process can crash after an external side effect succeeds but before its result is checkpointed. When recovery occurs, that step can execute again.

Therefore, demonstrating a restarted workflow is not sufficient to claim exactly-once business effects.

You still need idempotency, effect tracking, and external-outcome reconciliation.

I would rather show one completely reliable application-generation journey than an ambitious durability demonstration with an unproven edge case.

I would absolutely retain it as a strategic differentiator.

2. Starts a business workflow.
3. Records completed execution steps.

6. Reconciles uncertain external operations.

DBOS documents recovery from the last completed workflow step and durable sleeps and messaging.
